Game Narrative

Bad narrative is the most expensive thing you ship.

Not the writer. The writer is the cheapest line in the budget. Being wrong about the story is what costs a studio — and it is legible in preproduction, years before it shows up on an earnings call.

Structural diagnosis for games in production. Fixed scope, fixed fee, every claim sourced.

What Cuts Against This Page

Before anything else, because you would find it anyway and then you would stop reading.

The record, including the inconvenient parts

  • The Last of Us Part II sold over ten million copies and won Game of the Year at nearly every ceremony that gives one. That is a publisher-confirmed figure — copies, sold through. It is not a flop and I will not call it one.
  • Assassin’s Creed Shadows posted the second-highest day-one revenue in franchise history, absorbed eleven months of organised boycott, and Ubisoft has said it “delivered on its expectations.” A boycott moves a user score. It does not move a P&L.
  • Dragon Age: The Veilguard has an 82 on Metacritic with zero negative critic reviews, and its verified-purchase Steam score is positive. Its buyers are split. The internet is not. Those are different findings and they need different fixes.
  • None of these games was broken at launch. Part II shipped clean and won an accessibility award. Concord ran fine. Veilguard’s PC port was solid. Every one of them failed at a layer no QA pass tests for.
  • Roughly 45,000 games jobs were eliminated between 2022 and mid-2025, and by my own arithmetic on the public record, well under 1% of them are plausibly traceable to a content or story decision. One Swedish holding company’s debt problem accounts for 4,532 of them, 44 studios, and 80 cancelled projects. The industry’s contraction is a capital-markets story. Anyone selling you a simpler one is selling you something.

I lead with this because a diagnosis is worth exactly what its worst claim is worth. If I will shade a number to win an argument on a marketing page, you have no reason to trust the note I hand you about your second act.

Every figure on this page is sourced. Where the record is contested, I say so. Where a number is a rumour, I don’t use it. I will do the same with your game — including the parts of the criticism that are wrong.

The Autopsy

Seven failure modes, each one diagnosable from a script and a beat map before a line is recorded. This is the list I run against your game.

  1. The functionary protagonist

    A character built to occupy the role rather than to want something. Mamet’s memo to his writers is three questions per scene: who wants what, what happens if they don’t get it, why now. Andrew Stanton’s version is that every drawn character has “an inner motor… an itch they can’t scratch” — and, decisively, “these spines don’t always drive you to make the best choices.” A character built to be right cannot be driven into a wrong choice, and a character who cannot be wrong cannot generate a scene.

    The testFive playtesters, an index card, hour twelve: write down what the protagonist wants. If the cards disagree, you don’t have a protagonist, you have a camera.

  2. Theme announced instead of paid for

    A theme the player cannot disobey is a theme the player did not choose, and a player who did not choose it did not learn it. Kotaku on a game whose developer promised philosophical questions: “I never felt like the game asked me anything. Instead, it told me ‘brutality,’ repeatedly and louder, until by the end I couldn’t hear what it was trying to say at all.” That is from a review of a game that won Game of the Year.

    The testCount the lines of UI and dialogue that explain a consequence the staging already showed. Every one is a scene that failed playtest and got a caption instead of a rewrite. That number is your narrative debt.

  3. Consequence without complexity

    A decision with a consequence but no cost is a menu, not a dilemma. Branch counts are a production metric, not a drama metric — you can ship a thousand of them and never once make the player give something up.

    The testOne question per branch: what does the player lose that they wanted? If the answer is nothing, the branch is set dressing and the branching budget was spent on inventory.

  4. The sanded edge

    Friction removed until nothing remains that could be argued with, and therefore nothing that could be felt. A cross-industry workgroup put it plainly last year: the industry’s fearful reflexes “aim to sand down the rough edges of creative work, creating homogenous and bland works that check all the boxes but are nevertheless insincere, uninspiring and uninterested.” The same group’s finding on the other side: “courage is a consistent factor of most hit titles.”

    The testName one scene where a companion’s conviction costs the player something they wanted. If no companion is structurally obliged to disagree, you removed the only engine a party game has.

  5. Authorship by reorg

    No script holds one voice across five owners. One recent RPG passed through an executive producer, a creative director, a second executive producer, a production director, and a change of game director — plus a conversion to live service and back again — before anyone argued about a line of its dialogue. The seams always show in act two, because acts one and three get the executive attention.

    The testIn your own greenlight review: name the single person who owns the spine of this story, and give me the odds they are still here at lock. If you can’t, the rewrite is already scheduled. You just haven’t been told the date.

  6. The revision made out of fear

    A note that begins “so we don’t get what they got” is a schedule risk, not a creative direction. A tonal rewrite ordered late, to avoid a reception rather than to serve a story, produces exactly the reception it was meant to prevent — because a register change needs time to propagate and never gets it.

    The testDate every tonal change against your lock. Never accept one you cannot finish.

  7. The sequel that charges for affection

    Continuity is not a liability to be capped. It is the only asset a sequel owns that a new IP cannot buy. When a studio caps imported player history to avoid contradiction risk, it has decided to spend nothing on the players who invested the most — and the player who remembers least becomes the player the game is built for. The two most-upvoted negative reviews of one reboot, posted the same day: “The developers literally did everything fans told them not to do,” and “Look how they massacred our boy.”

    The testA three-question import screen is a balance-sheet decision wearing a design costume. Price it as one.

The Variable Is Not Who Is In The Game

The most useful thing in this analysis, and the part that makes it a service rather than an opinion.

The same years that produced those failures produced Baldur’s Gate 3, Clair Obscur: Expedition 33, and Metaphor: ReFantazio. Every feature the failures were attacked for is present in the winners, usually more of it. Baldur’s Gate 3 has thirteen romance options with no gender gating, full pronoun and body decoupling, and a GLAAD award — and it is the most awarded game ever made. Metaphor is an explicit anti-bigotry text and took Best Narrative. So the demographics of a cast cannot be the variable. Something else separates these two lists, and it is structural.

What the winners do

The diverse element carries structural weight and zero moral weight. Cut it and the plot collapses — a boy’s apostasy is the cult plot; prejudice is a numeric obstacle to winning the election; the shadow self is the boss you cannot beat.

And yet no character’s identity is ever the source of their moral authority. Allegiance is earned the ordinary way: they want something concrete — find my mother, get off this mountain, save my marriage — and they pay for it.

What the failures do

Both terms inverted, exactly. The identity content is structurally weightless — delete it and the plot runs unchanged, which is the definition of decoration.

And it is morally load-bearing — it is the character’s claim on the audience’s approval and the game’s implicit case for its own virtue. An attribute stated for a moment of sympathy and then given nothing to do is a dropped setup. It reads as inventory rather than character, no matter what the attribute is.

That inversion is what audiences detect, and it is a craft fault rather than a political one: a want the writer would not let the character have, and a thesis the writer would not let the player argue with. It is also the charge that cannot be deflected — it says the writing under-serves the trait, which is the opposite of objecting to the trait’s presence.

The strike test

Cut every line where a character’s identity is discussed rather than exercised. Then ask two questions.

Does the plot still function? If yes, the identity was bolted on, and your audience already felt the join.
Is the theme still arguable? If no, you didn’t write a story. You wrote a lecture, and you are about to charge sixty dollars for it.

This takes an afternoon. It costs nothing. Almost nobody runs it.

A sharp CFO will push back, and they will be right: story is the cheapest thing in a game to change late. Words are free to retype. The expensive churn is engineering.

That is true, and it is not the argument. The argument is that a rotten premise silently mis-directs every expensive department for years — the level layouts built for a beat that gets cut, the mocap shot for a scene that no longer works, the systems built to serve a theme nobody in the building can state in one sentence. Bad craft does not stop launch week; marketing buys launch week. It eats the DLC, the sequel, the franchise, and the studio.

Sony’s President and COO named the process failure on the record after the shortest-lived first-party launch in the company’s history: “We probably need to have a lot of gates, including user testing or internal evaluation… we should have done those gates much earlier than we did. Also, we have a siloed organisation.”

Hiroki Totoki, Sony Group Q2 FY2024 earnings call, 8 November 2024, and Sony’s own published Q&A summary the same day.

Nobody knows anything. Goldman’s line is the most famous maxim in entertainment and a direct refutation of anyone selling insurance against commercial failure — so let me be exact about what is on offer. I cannot tell you what will succeed. Nobody can, and the ones who say otherwise are the reason your last focus group cost six figures and produced a cast nobody remembers.

I can tell you, in week eight rather than year four, that your protagonist has no want, that your theme is being announced rather than dramatised, and that your sequel is charging the audience for the privilege of being corrected. Those are diagnosable. Whether the fix sells is not, and saying so is the only reason to believe the rest of this page.
